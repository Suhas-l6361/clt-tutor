Read the passage below and answer Questions 1–5.
For most of the twentieth century, the public library was imagined as a quiet storehouse of books, a place where silence was enforced and borrowing was the main activity. That picture is now out of date. In many Indian cities, libraries that once stood half-empty have begun to fill again, not because people have suddenly rediscovered printed books, but because the library has quietly changed its purpose.
Today a well-run library is as likely to host a coding workshop or a session on filling in government forms as a reading circle. Students who lack a quiet room at home use its tables to prepare for competitive examinations; job-seekers use its computers to apply for work; elderly residents come for the newspapers and the company. In this sense, the library has become one of the few public spaces where a person can spend hours without being expected to buy anything.
Critics argue that this shift dilutes the library's original mission. If libraries become community centres, they say, the patient work of building and caring for collections will be neglected, and the reader who wants a rare book will be left behind. The concern is not unreasonable: budgets are limited, and every rupee spent on a computer is a rupee not spent on books.
Yet the choice may be less stark than it appears. A library that draws people in for one purpose often turns them into readers for another. The student who comes for a quiet desk notices the shelves; the parent who brings a child to a storytelling session leaves with a borrowed novel. The library's future, then, may depend less on defending its old identity than on recognising that access to knowledge has always meant more than access to books.
